The Harness · Part 06

Operating the Harness

A program owner's guide, for the people who staff, fund, govern and measure agent programs.

~13 MIN READUPDATED OCT 20261 LIVE INSTRUMENT

Of 1,340 teams LangChain surveyed at the end of 2025, 89% had observability on their agents. Only 52.4% ran offline evals, and 37.3% scored live traffic.1 Most programs can see what their agents did. Far fewer can say whether it was right. That gap is not a model problem. It is an operating problem, and it belongs to whoever owns the budget, the team and the sign-off. This part is for that person.

Buy the inner harness, own the outer one

The number budget owners quote is MIT NANDA's: external partnerships reached deployment about 67% of the time, internally built tools about 33%.2 Read it before you quote it. It rests on 52 organization interviews, 153 surveyed leaders and a review of 300+ public initiatives. The authors call the rates “self-reported” and warn that the correlation “does not necessarily prove causation”.2 Teams that buy may simply pick narrower problems.

The counter-signal is just as real. McKinsey's 2026 survey, as reported by The Register, found nearly a third of respondents had decided against buying at least one software product because agentic coding tools let them build it.3 And a16z found CIOs shifting toward buying, with 37% now using five or more models.4

Build vs buy is the wrong unit. Ask it per subsystem. MIT's own diagnosis of the failures is a “learning gap”: tools that do not learn, integrate poorly, or do not match the workflow.2 Those are outer-harness properties, in Böckeler's sense: the instruction files, tools and checks you add around the vendor's agent.5 No vendor can ship your workflow. The 33% are, plausibly, teams that tried to build both layers.

The rule in one line: buy the parts that are the same for every customer, own the parts that are your business.

Who owns what

Two failure shapes recur. Every team builds its own sandbox, tracing and policy engine, badly and five times. Or a central team builds a grand platform nobody adopted. Team Topologies names the middle path, the thinnest viable platform.6

“That might just be a wiki page - that might be your platform.”— Matthew Skelton, Team Topologies

For agents, the thin platform owns the inner-harness primitives: the sandbox image, the permission-policy engine, tracing, the eval runner, and a shared registry of skills and tools. Embedded engineers, sitting with each use case, own that use case's outer harness: its context, tool contracts, policy rules and eval tasks. This is the internal version of the forward-deployed engineer from the prologue, and the same test applies: does a field fix become a reusable artifact?

Staff evals first. OpenAI's first lesson from seven enterprise deployments is “Start with evals”; Morgan Stanley ran them for every use case before rollout.7 Thoughtworks' CTO puts the staffing argument plainly.8

“There's a significant need for humans to proactively implement appropriate practices and technical harnesses.”— Rachel Laycock, Thoughtworks (2026)

The maturity ladder

Autonomy is a setting you choose, not a property the model has. The Knight Institute's levels define it by the user's role: Operator, Collaborator, Consultant, Approver, Observer.9 In their words, autonomy “can instead be a deliberate design decision made by agent developers.”9 The Cloud Security Alliance's Agentic Trust Framework adds what most ladders lack: promotion gates. An agent observes for at least two weeks, recommends with approval for four weeks at over 95% accuracy, then acts with notification after eight weeks without a critical incident, and is demoted on incident.10

METR's data explains why gates must use measured reliability. The task length models finish 50% of the time doubles about every seven months, and the 80%-reliability horizon is much shorter.11 Demos show the 50% horizon. Production runs on the other one.

Field note · for leadersBefore you approve a promotion up the ladder, ask for three things: the stage it is in now and the gate evidence for the next one (weeks observed, accuracy, incidents), the permission tier it will run under, and who gets paged when it misbehaves. If any answer is a promise rather than a dated record, the agent stays where it is.

Cost and routing

An agent's bill is a loop, so model it as one. Per task: cost ≈ turns × (cached prefix × read price + new tokens × write price + output tokens × output price). As of October 2026, Anthropic bills cache writes at 1.25× the base input price for a five-minute lifetime or 2× for an hour, and reads at 0.1×, with newer models lower still at 0.025× to 0.05×.16 Manus reports input running about 100 tokens for every output token and calls cache hit rate “the single most important metric for a production-stage AI agent.”17

The formula says two things. First, the cache is structural, not a tuning knob. An append-only loop reads its prefix every turn and writes only the new suffix, so a high hit rate is the default; what breaks it is a mutated prefix or an idle gap past the cache lifetime. Second, turns dominate. Anthropic's multi-agent research system beat a single agent by 90.2% on its internal eval, but used about 15 times the tokens of chat, and token usage alone explained 80% of performance variance.18 Fan-out buys quality with money. Decide that on purpose.

Route by job. That same system used a frontier lead to plan and a smaller model for its workers.18 Cursor screens commands with a small classifier model.19 The rule of thumb: small, fast models for classifiers, summarizers, compaction and well-specified subagents; the frontier model for planning and for the step whose error is expensive. Check each route against the eval suite, not against intuition.

Then cap it. Anthropic recommends stopping conditions such as a maximum number of iterations.12 In a program, make that a per-task hard budget in tokens and dollars, enforced by the harness, which aborts the run and records why. A cap the model can talk past is a suggestion.

Getting it past the risk review

Risk reviewers do not want a demo. They want a page that answers their questions in their vocabulary. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework gives that vocabulary: four functions, Govern, Map, Measure and Manage, with a generative-AI profile, NIST AI 600-1, published in July 2024; the framework is being revised.14 NIST's agent standards initiative, launched February 2026, is working on agent identity and authorization, but no final standard exists yet.20 ISO/IEC 42001 is the certifiable management-system standard, built on the plan-do-check-act structure of ISO 27001.21

The EU AI Act dates moved this year. Per the Future of Life Institute's tracker (a secondary source), the Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026, pushes Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and Annex I product obligations to 2 August 2028. Article 50 transparency duties stayed on 2 August 2026.22 Check the primary text before you plan against it.

The pack maps cleanly. The inventory entry and trifecta analysis are Map. Eval evidence is Measure. The kill switch, rollback and incident owner are Manage. The named sponsor is Govern. Threat-model it against the OWASP agentic Top 10, from goal hijack (ASI01) and tool misuse (ASI02) to rogue agents (ASI10).15

Field note · for leadersBefore you approve an agent for production, ask for the pack above and read section 3 first. Every line there should name a test that proves the control holds. A control described only in the system prompt is a request to the model, and the review should treat it as missing.

What goes on the exec dashboard

Define the metric before you compare. Klarna's first month in February 2024: 2.3 million conversations, two-thirds of service chats, the work of 700 full-time agents, resolution time down from 11 minutes to under 2, and a projected $40 million profit improvement.23 In May 2025, Fortune reported, from an interview with Bloomberg, that the CEO said quality had suffered and the company was hiring human agents again.24

“As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant evaluation factor when organizing this, what you end up having is lower quality.”— Sebastian Siemiatkowski, via Fortune (2025)

Intercom reports a 76% average resolution rate for Fin, and is now moving from counting resolutions to counting outcomes, which include correct handoffs to humans.25 Same agent, new definition, different number. LangChain's survey shows teams already know quality is the problem: it is the top barrier for about a third of respondents, and 59.8% use human review.1

The gen_ai.* names come from the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions.26 Tag every span with the harness version, or no number on this board can be tied to a change.

ROI follows from the board. Value = tasks × success rate × value per task − (tokens × price + engineering time + review time). Reliability is the term most business cases miss. If pass^k is unmeasured, every output needs a human, and review cost scales with volume. If pass^k at your k is measured and high, review drops to a sample.27 An illustrative month (made-up inputs, to show the shape): 10,000 tasks at 80% success and $15 of avoided work each is $120,000. Subtract $4,000 of tokens and $30,000 of engineering. Reviewing every task at $3 costs $30,000 and leaves $56,000. Sampling 10% leaves $83,000. The difference is the eval suite.

Tie the board to the P&L, not to anecdotes. McKinsey's 2026 survey, per The Register, found 80% of AI users reporting better personal productivity while the share attributing EBIT impact to AI stayed flat at 37%.3

When it fires anyway

An agent with write access will eventually do something nobody approved. The CSA framework lists the controls to have before that day: a manual kill switch that acts in under a second, a circuit breaker that halts on repeated failures, revocation of every agent session, and state rollback.10 OpenAI's guide adds the triggers to escalate on: failure thresholds such as retry limits, and actions that are sensitive, irreversible or high-stakes.13

Runbook · agent incident
  1. Stop. Hit the kill switch for the agent, not the whole platform. Confirm no session is still dispatching tools.
  2. Revoke. Rotate the agent's credentials and tokens in one action. It has its own identity, so humans keep working.
  3. Replay. Pull the trace and the policy decision log for the session. Find the first tool call that should have been denied.
  4. Tell. The named comms owner updates affected users and the sponsor. Roll back to the last harness version that passed the gate.
  5. Encode. Turn the failure into an eval case in the regression set and a hard control in the pack. The agent goes down one stage until it passes.

The whole harness, on one page

Every box below is a chapter. Take it into the design review; click through for the evidence.

The book started from one equation. Vivek Trivedy at LangChain wrote it down as Agent = Model + Harness, the harness being “every piece of code, configuration, and execution logic that isn't the model itself.”28 Birgitta Böckeler split it into the inner harness a vendor ships and the outer harness you build.5 Five chapters took each part of the harness apart. This one added the people and the paperwork that let it run on a Tuesday with nobody watching.

The model is the part you rent. The harness is the part you own, and operating it is a management job as much as an engineering one: a thin platform, embedded owners, autonomy earned by measured gates, a one-page pack the reviewers can sign, and a board that ties reliability to money.
